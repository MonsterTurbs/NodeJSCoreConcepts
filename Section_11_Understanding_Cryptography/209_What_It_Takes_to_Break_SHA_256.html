💥Avalanche Effect

Even changing one bit in your input totally scrambles the SHA-256 hash. You can’t tweak the message to predictably nudge the hash—brute force is the only way. 🔀

⛏️Brute-Forcing Example

A script loops a counter into your data, hashes it, and checks for a pattern (e.g. leading zeros). Finding 2 hex zeros (~8 zero bits) takes ~2⁸ ≈ 256 tries on average; 6 hex zeros (~24 bits) ~2²⁴ (~16 million) tries. 🎲

💰Bitcoin Mining Analogy

Miners adjust a “nonce” to find a SHA-256 hash under a target (lots of leading zeros). It’s pure brute force—no shortcuts thanks to avalanche effect. 🖥️🔒

🔑Preimage & 2nd-Preimage Attacks

• Preimage: Given a hash, find any input → ~2²⁵⁶ tries (impossible)
• 2nd-Preimage: Given one input & its hash, find another input with same hash → also ~2²⁵⁶ tries in SHA-256 ✅

🎁Collision & Birthday Attack

• Collision = two different inputs hashing equal.
• Birthday paradox reduces effort: ~2¹²⁸ work for SHA-256, still infeasible today. 🎂

📦Pigeonhole Principle

More possible inputs than 256-bit outputs → infinite collisions guaranteed. Yet none known found in SHA-256 after decades! 🕵️‍♂️

🌈Rainbow Tables

Precomputed tables map common passwords ↔ their hashes. Speeds lookup vs brute force—use long, unique salts to defeat them! 🔐

⏱️Password Length vs Crack Time

• Tiny inputs (32 bits) → 2³² tries → milliseconds
• 100 bits (≈12 chars) → 2¹⁰⁰ tries → millions of years

🚫Don’t Plain-Hash Passwords

Use salted, slow hashing (PBKDF2, bcrypt, Argon2)—never raw SHA-256 on passwords! 🛡️

⚛️Quantum Threat

Grover’s algorithm cuts effort to ~2¹²⁸ for collision search—still huge but shows SHA-256 isn’t quantum-proof. SHA-512 or post-quantum hashes may be needed later. 🌐

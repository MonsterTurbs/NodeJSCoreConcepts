📜 A Brief History of AES

🔐Before AES: DES

Data Encryption Standard (DES) used a 56-bit key and 64-bit blocks. Once secure, it became vulnerable to brute-force by the late ’90s and is now obsolete.

🏁The AES Competition

  • 1997: NIST invited global submissions to replace DES.
  • 15 algorithms entered; evaluated for security, speed, and simplicity.
  • 2001: Rijndael chosen as the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES).

⚖️Key Requirements

  • Security: Resistant to attacks for decades.
  • Efficiency: Fast in software & hardware (support for acceleration).
  • Simplicity: Easy to analyze and verify by cryptographers.

🚀Timeline

  • Early 1970s: DES developed by IBM & NSA.
  • 1977: DES adopted as U.S. federal standard.
  • 1997: NIST launches AES contest.
  • 2001: AES (Rijndael) becomes U.S. and global standard.

🏛️Government & Trust

Unlike DES (developed with NSA), AES was chosen via open international competition to build public trust. Ongoing debates about backdoors highlight encryption’s importance and sensitivity.

🔍Looking Forward

Changing standards takes years—if AES ever becomes vulnerable, its successor will emerge through careful study and public review, just like its origin.
